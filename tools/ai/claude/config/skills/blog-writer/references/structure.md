# Structure

The Structure is the list of parts of an article and their order.

## Parts

1. **Hook.** Opens the article with a concrete thing the Reader recognizes.
2. **Problem.** Shows what goes wrong or what is hard.
3. **Resolution.** Fixes the problem step by step.
4. **Close.** Ends the article briefly.

A short note may have only the hook and the resolution.

The four parts are a reference, not a mold. An article may also follow
chronological order, the order of events, with headings that follow the story.
Thematic articles stay valid.

### Hook

- One to five sentences. Open with a task, an error, a question, a wrong belief,
  an event or a situation.
- The Reader learns the problem or the situation in the first 3 sentences.
- The article may be answer-first or story-first. Pick the one that fits the
  material.
- A scene is optional. One or two sentences of context are enough.

### Problem

- Show a real attempt that fails, not only a description.
- Skip the obvious approach when the Reader already knows it.
- Say which alternatives you rejected, and why they failed.

### Resolution

- Prefer one example that grows. Each step fixes the flaw of the previous step.
- Put code or output right after the claim it supports. When the article says "I
  wrote a script that does X", the code sits right after that sentence. Not
  every article needs a code block.
- Name the idea once the Reader has seen it: "that is X".
- Vary the way you mark a turn in the story. Do not use the same phrase in every
  article.

### Close

- Two to five sentences, or fewer for a short note.
- End on the result or on what changes now, when there is one.
- A daily article may have nothing new to conclude. Then stop after the last
  step.
- No recap in short articles.
- No call to action by default. A soft "try it" is allowed when it is true.

## Headings

- Use a heading for each part when the article has two or more distinct parts.
  Headings help the Reader scan.
- Skip headings in a note with one idea and a few paragraphs.
- Keep headings short and plain: a label, a step or a question. "The workaround"
  is a good heading. In an article that follows the order of events, a heading
  may name a moment: "The crash", "Every two minutes".
- Put the first heading after the intro.

## Pacing

- Paragraphs of 1 to 4 sentences. Sections of 50 to 250 words.
- One-line paragraphs work as pauses.
- A question after a step moves the story to the next step.
- Use a bullet list for parallel items only. Use bold labels in lists.
